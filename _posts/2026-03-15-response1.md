Response 1: On the Importance of Context for Visual Communication
When a Visualization Looks Like It Already Knows the Answer 

There is something strangely persuasive about a visualization. The moment you see a heatmap or a word cloud it almost feels like the explanation is already there waiting for you. The colors look deliberate, the shapes look organized, and the numbers seem confident enough that you almost trust them immediately. Before you have even begun thinking about interpretation the graph already feels like it has a story to tell. This assignment pushed me to question that feeling by asking what happens when a large language model tries to interpret our visualizations without knowing the context behind them. What I discovered is that visualizations can appear extremely clear while still hiding the assumptions that make them meaningful.

In the first assignment, my project partner and I created several visualizations using a small corpus of Harry Potter texts, combining two canonical novels with three fanfiction works. The goal was to explore emotional language across the texts. For this response, however, I decided that I wanted the focus to shift away from the corpus itself and toward a different question: what happens when an LLM tries to interpret those visualizations without knowing anything about them?

To explore this I uploaded screenshots of several graphs from our analysis to an LLM called ArabyGPT and asked what the images showed. I intentionally avoided explaining the dataset so that the model would interpret the visuals on its own, and I asked the questions in Arabic just to see how the interpretation might change when the prompt itself came from a different language.

The explanation the model produced sounded confident almost immediately, regardless of the language in which the prompts were written. It described the heatmap as a comparison of how often certain characters and emotional words appear across Harry Potter texts. It also interpreted the word cloud as highlighting the most important characters and narrative themes of the series. On the surface the explanation felt convincing. Harry appears most frequently across the corpus and the LLM correctly connected that to his role as the central protagonist. Hermione, Ron, and Dumbledore also appear prominently which fits with their narrative importance.

The interesting part is not what the model noticed but what it could not see. The LLM did not know that the corpus included both canonical novels and fanfiction texts written in very different contexts. It also did not know that the documents vary dramatically in length. One of the Rowling novels contains well over one hundred thousand words while some of the fanfiction texts are far shorter. Because of this difference longer texts naturally produce larger raw word counts. When the visualization shows higher frequencies in the longer novel the LLM reads that difference as stronger thematic emphasis rather than as a structural feature of the dataset. The graph itself is accurate but the interpretation shifts depending on what the viewer knows about the corpus.

A similar issue appears in the visualizations that track emotional vocabulary. When the chart shows that the word love appears frequently in certain texts the LLM interprets this as evidence that love dominates the emotional world of the story. This interpretation is not entirely wrong since love is a major theme in the Harry Potter series. At the same time literature rarely communicates emotion through isolated keywords alone. A scene can feel terrifying without the word fear appearing anywhere on the page and characters can express anger without repeating the word anger directly. The visualization highlights patterns in vocabulary but it cannot capture the emotional atmosphere of the narrative itself.

Comparing the LLM interpretation with my own reading of the graphs made this difference especially noticeable. As a human reader I approach the visualization already knowing the structure of the corpus. I know which texts are canonical novels and which are fanfiction and I know the approximate length of each document. Because of that knowledge the graph becomes more like a map that helps confirm or challenge expectations. The LLM approaches the same visualization very differently. It treats the image as if it contains all the relevant information and assumes that the patterns it sees must fully explain the story. The explanation is logically consistent but it lacks the contextual assumptions that shape human interpretation.

This experience connects directly to the argument made in the Visualization Design Lab article “Misleading Visualizations,” which explains that visual representations often appear objective even though they depend heavily on how data is selected and presented. When viewers do not know how the data was structured they can easily draw conclusions that seem logical but overlook important limitations. Watching the LLM interpret my graphs showcased that exact idea.

Ted Underwood raises a similar concern in his discussion of distant reading where he warns that computational analysis can create an illusion of objectivity. When literature is translated into numbers and graphs the results can look precise even though interpretation remains essential. The visualizations from our Harry Potter corpus helped reveal patterns across a large collection of texts, but understanding what those patterns actually mean still required context and human interpretation.

In the end the experiment revealed something simple but important. Visualizations are powerful tools for noticing patterns, but they do not explain themselves. Without context even a confident graph or a confident AI can produce explanations that sound convincing while missing the bigger picture.
